Moses destroyed a golden calf because Israel worshipped it.
Centuries later, King Hezekiah destroyed another object Israel had begun worshipping.
But this one had been made at God's command.
And that raises a strange question.
How could something God Himself commanded become an idol?
In Numbers 21, deadly serpents were killing the Israelites.
The people confessed their sin and begged God to remove them.
But God's answer was unexpected.
He commanded Moses to raise a serpent on a pole. Anyone who had been bitten could look at it and live.
Notice something.
They weren't told to worship the serpent.
They weren't told to make offerings to it.
They were told to look—and live.
Yet centuries later, according to Second Kings 18, the Israelites were doing exactly what God had never commanded.
They were making offerings to the serpent itself.
The object hadn't changed.
But its purpose had been reversed.
What once directed people toward God's provision had become an object of worship.
So Hezekiah smashed it.
And here's where the story becomes extraordinary.
Centuries after its destruction, Jesus brings up that very serpent while speaking to Nicodemus.
He says:
"As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up."
He was speaking of His coming death on the cross.
In the wilderness, dying Israelites looked at what God provided and received life.
Now Jesus reveals a greater fulfillment.
Those who believe in Him may receive eternal life.
And just two verses after Jesus mentions the serpent comes one of the most famous verses in Scripture.
John 3:16.
Now look at the extraordinary irony.
The Israelites preserved the serpent, but lost sight of its purpose.
Hezekiah destroyed the serpent.
Yet Jesus revealed the meaning that survived its destruction.
What Israel had turned into an object of worship, Jesus used to reveal the way to eternal life.
The bronze serpent was never the ultimate source of their salvation.
And its destruction couldn't destroy the truth it pointed toward.
Because the story was never ultimately about the serpent.
It was pointing toward Christ.
