## 1. The job
A reel script is a retention machine: every line must make the viewer need the next one (Kallaway's "domino effect"). One question, one answer, one feeling. If a line doesn't grab, advance the argument, or set up a payoff, cut it.

## 2. What The Lintel Network actually does (from the two transcripts we have)
"Why didn't God remove the tree?" (7.4M) and "Where did Cain's wife come from?" (1.7M):
- HOOK = a question real people secretly ask, phrased as a tension or an objection ("If God knew Eve would eat the fruit, why didn't He remove the tree?"). It is the viewer's question, not the creator's thesis.
- STAKES = restate the tension in concrete, escalating terms ("He knew every war and every sickness... and still He left the tree"), or social stakes ("most asked... most Christians can't answer it").
- BODY = a reasoning chain the viewer can follow, not a retelling. Connectives do the work: "If... so... which means... but notice... the clue is...". Scripture is used as EVIDENCE (a verse card is a clue), never as a story recap.
- THOUGHT EXPERIMENT + PARALLEL EXAMPLES in threes ("Thou shalt not steal -> He'd have to take away everything you could be tempted to take. Thou shalt not covet -> ..."). The viewer feels the logic.
- THE HEART: one emotional line that turns logic into love ("He wanted children who love Him freely"; "every person you've ever met is family").
- THE CONNECTION (aha): a satisfying link that closes the loop ("sin came through a tree; Jesus paid on a second tree").
- ANSWER: the hook's question repeated, answered in one line ("So why didn't God remove the tree? Because love has to be chosen...").
- CTA: a binary comment question ("gift or burden?") or social currency ("next time someone asks, you'll know what to say").
- STYLE: plain words, short lines, one key word per line in caps on screen, one or two verse cards, about 85-90 s.
What they do NOT do: retell the story, list facts for their own sake, or preach a thesis before the viewer cares.

## 3. Craft rules (master level)
1. Start from the viewer's question, not my point. A hook is the viewer's doubt, fear or curiosity, said in their words.
2. Hook formula (Kallaway): context lean-in (the topic, in common ground) -> interjection ("but") -> snapback (the opposite of what they expect). Staccato sentences in the hook; vary the length after.
3. Speed to value: a hit of value within about 4 seconds of the hook.
4. Stakes: make the question matter (it's about you, your faith, your life).
5. Story is evidence, not content: use only the moments that prove the next step of the argument, each in one line, while the visuals show the rest.
6. Reasoning chain with "but / therefore / which means" between beats, never "and then". Each beat opens a small question that the next beat answers (rehooks).
7. Make abstract truths concrete with a thought experiment or three parallel examples.
8. One emotional core line. People share feeling plus meaning, not information.
9. Save the biggest payoff for the end; give small payoffs along the way.
10. Land: echo the hook's exact words, answer in one plain sentence, end on a line worth sending.
11. CTA: one, short, tied to the value (a comment question or "next time someone asks..."), never "like and follow".
12. Write for mute: the on-screen words alone must carry it.
13. One idea per reel. If the lesson needs four sentences of explanation, the structure is wrong.

## 4. Why my Samson drafts failed
- They opened with MY thesis ("Your gift working doesn't mean God approves..."), a sermon point, not a question the viewer is already asking.
- They retold the story beat by beat, then explained the lesson. Lintel argues the answer and uses the story as evidence.
- The lesson was asserted, not reasoned: no thought experiment, no concrete examples, no emotional core.
- Too many ideas at once (drift, the trap, God's purpose, the verdict, results), so nothing landed.
- The CTA was generic.
Candidate fit for Samson in the Lintel shape: the question people actually ask, "Why did God keep giving Samson strength when he kept sinning?" Answer by reasoning (whose purpose the strength served; what that means about God's power on a life), with "he did not know that the LORD had left him" as the turn, and a heart line plus a mirror question ("would you know?"). Wait for the Lintel scripts before writing.

## 5. Teardown template for the scripts Marcus sends
For each reel: views, length, words, words per second.
Per line: time · words · JOB (hook / stakes / evidence / reasoning / example / proof verse / heart / connection / answer / CTA) · device (question, contrast, parallel three, thought experiment, caps keyword, callback).
Then tally: % of time on story vs reasoning vs heart vs CTA; how fast the first value lands; number of rehooks; how the answer echoes the hook; CTA type.
Output: one blueprint, with the line-level jobs, that every Fourth Watch script follows.

## 6. Teardown: Lintel "What happens to people who never heard of Jesus?" (1.6M, 137 s, 396 words, about 173 wpm)
- HOOK + SHARPEN + FRAME (13%, ~17 s): the question from their comments; a concrete person ("a man born in a remote village") plus the doctrinal tension ("If Jesus is the only way..."); "let's allow the Bible to answer it."
- STORY AS EVIDENCE (22%, ~30 s): ONE story that IS the exact case (Cornelius: never heard, yet feared God), told in one paragraph with specifics (Caesarea, Roman officer, angel, Joppa by the sea), ending on a quote that answers the question (Acts 10:34-35).
- ANSWER IN 3 NUMBERED POINTS (65%, ~90 s), escalating:
  1. What the story shows, in one line (God sent the gospel to his door).
  2. The wider Bible picture, the longest part (46%): several witnesses stacked (Paul, Romans 1 and 2; the altar "to the unknown God" in Athens; Jesus, Luke 12:48; Abraham and Sodom, Gen 18:25, the ten), then honest nuance (Christians disagree; the main views in one line) and the firm common ground (no one is saved except through Jesus).
  3. Flip the question onto the viewer ("it's also who is going to tell them"), plus a concrete, warm action CTA ("think of the person who first told you about Jesus and say thank you"), which also drives shares.
- Devices: numbered points; cool facts as evidence (the altar, the bargain down to ten); honest about disagreement, firm on the core; the story is used as proof, never as content.

## 7. Teardown: Lintel "If God is love, why is there a hell?" (~97 s)
- HOOK: the question, sharpened ("...send anyone there forever?"), plus a relatability line ("Some Christians also wonder...").
- NO story section. Straight to three numbered points.
- Each point = an imperative opener ("Ask who...", "Look at who...") -> Scripture as evidence -> one plain line of meaning -> a REHOOK QUESTION that the next point answers ("Why does anyone end up there?", "Does God force anyone either way?", "Why is there a hell then?").
- One sticky image line ("He knocks but he never kicks the door in"), called back in the answer ("He is still knocking") and in the CTA.
- The final point answers the hook's question explicitly, with the gospel.
- CTA: a comment question built on the reel's image.

## 8. THE BLUEPRINT (from both Lintel scripts)
1. HOOK: the question people actually ask, sharpened with the tension or a concrete case, plus a relatability line ("People ask this all the time; let's let the Bible answer it").
2. STORY AS EVIDENCE (optional, at most ~25% of the reel): one paragraph, specifics, ending on the quote that matters.
3. THREE NUMBERED POINTS (~60%+ of the reel), escalating. Each: an imperative or claim opener -> Scripture as evidence -> one plain, memorable line -> a rehook question leading to the next point.
   - Point 2 is the deepest: stacked witnesses and cool facts, honest nuance where Christians differ, firm on the core.
   - Point 3 turns it to the viewer and/or the gospel and answers the hook in one line, with a callback to the reel's image.
4. CTA: one line, either a comment question on the reel's image or a warm, concrete action.

## 9. Teardown: Lintel "Methuselah / why so short now?" (screen recording from Marcus, 99.6 s; video only, no audio captured)
Script reconstructed from the burned-in captions:
- 0–5 s: a counter runs 173 → 864 → 969 under a figure in a spotlight; "969 YEARS OLD". Hook question with the big title at the bottom: "And why did God make our lives so much SHORTER?" / WHY SO SHORT NOW?
- 9–20 s: surprising fact as evidence: "Methuselah lived 969 years, and the year he died tells us something remarkable about God. Add up the ages in Genesis, and Methuselah dies in the very year the FLOOD came." (animated timeline chart, Genesis 5)
- 21–30 s: meaning: "God let the longest life in history run its full course before judgment fell." + 1 Peter 3:20 card ("the LONGSUFFERING of God WAITED").
- 31–47 s: the second question and the answer: "So why did God shorten human life after that? The answer starts in Genesis chapter 6." Genesis 6:3 card.
- 48–58 s: data: "After the flood, lifespans fall steadily, from Noah's 950 years to Abraham's 175, to Moses at 120." (animated bars with figures)
- 59–67 s: honest nuance: "Christians read that verse two ways. Some see a new LIMIT... others see a COUNTDOWN of 120 years until the flood." (split screen, counter 120 → 0)
- 68–83 s: application: "consider what a shorter life does. Evil has less time to grow in any one HEART, and ETERNITY feels closer to every one of us." + Psalm 90:12 card.
- 84–90 s: GOSPEL LANDING: "God shortened our days on earth, but through JESUS, He offers a life that will NEVER end."
- 91–98 s: CTA: a genuine either/or: "If human life was still 900 years long today, do you think the world would be BETTER, or far WORSE? Tell us in the comments." BETTER / WORSE buttons appear on their words; a comment box types "Honestly, worse, becau…".
Visual grammar: a new picture every 2–4 s; flat minimal 2D/iso scenes with spotlight, dust motes, vignette; narration top-left in italic serif, key words in red (warning) or gold caps; big numbers/titles in the lower third; a dark pill at the bottom naming the IDEA of each scene (A SHORTER LIFE, LESS TIME TO GROW, A LIFE THAT NEVER ENDS, JUDGMENT); verse cards are light parchment with dark text and red key words; data graphics (timeline, bars, countdown, hourglass, heart, candle) carry the facts.
Takeaways for Fourth Watch: (1) faster visual change, with graphic inserts inside long shots; (2) concept pills naming the idea; (3) data graphics as evidence; (4) always land on Jesus right before the CTA; (5) the CTA is a real either/or, shown as buttons, with a comment being typed.
