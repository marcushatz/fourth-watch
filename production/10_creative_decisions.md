# 10 · Creative decisions: choosing and shaping a story

**Use this before any script.** It decides whether an idea deserves production. It runs inside the script stage and adds no approval.

**Why it exists (Marcus, Round 39):** "Our mission is not to make viewers think, 'That was an interesting Bible fact.' Our mission is to make them think, 'I never realized that. That was incredible. I want to watch another.'"

**Evidence:** `research/creative_study_2026-11-03/findings.md`. It's small. Our own results replace it as soon as we have them.

## What changes

A story starts with a person and a feeling. The biblical detail comes second, and it has to deepen the experience.

Curiosity or conflict gets people in. Emotion is what they remember. Comfort alone earns love but little reach, and a list of connections earns neither.

## Step 1: the idea card

Copy `templates/idea_card.md` into the episode folder. Every box needs an answer that could be checked. There are no scores. They were judgment dressed up as measurement.

| Question | A real answer | A warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Who specifically cares? | A kind of person, plus proof they're looking | "Christians who like typology" |
| 2. What will they feel? | One starting feeling and one destination, both from the text | "Interesting" |
| 3. What will they newly understand? | "Before, they think this. After, they see that" | A fact with no before |
| 4. Why is the middle as rewarding as the opening? | A human turn: a reversal, a choice or a cost | A stack of references |
| 5. Why is the ending bigger than the first discovery? | It changes what the opening meant, using something planted | A recap or a moral |
| 6. Why would someone share it? | "I'd send this to ___ because ___" | "People would find it cool" |
| 7. Why would someone follow afterward? | The promise it kept, and the next video they'd want | "It's good content" |

**Judge four things separately:** the concept, this particular script, what Scripture supports, and production feasibility. A weak draft is not a weak concept. Label what comes from real audiences, your own judgment and speculation.

**The hook can only promise what Scripture provides.** If the text doesn't give a motive, don't build the episode on revealing it. Separate what the text states, what it implies and what interpreters propose.

## Step 2: the two-track outline

Choose the structure the story needs: several revelations, or one moment it all builds to. Lintel's structure is one option, not a formula. The climax can be the ending, so don't add explanation after it.

Establish what the person wants, what they fear, what stands in the way, what happens and why it matters. The feeling should come from those events.

Five to seven beats. Each beat has three columns: what they learn, what they feel, why they stay.

- No discovery quota. One surprising detail can be enough, and each one has to change the feeling.
- One line written to be the peak.
- One deliberate destination.
- The ending should pay off something already in the story.

| Destination | Honest source | How it gets faked |
|---|---|---|
| Awe and wonder | Scale, or something larger than expected | Calling it "incredible" |
| Heartbreak or compassion | A specific person's loss, told plainly | Sad music over a general statement |
| Joy and hope | A real reversal the story has earned | Promises the text doesn't make |
| Encouragement and courage | Someone ordinary acting while afraid | Telling viewers they can do it |
| Suspense and anticipation | A clock, a danger or a withheld outcome | Hiding what's obvious |
| Astonishment | A plain detail that overturns what they assumed | Calling it a secret or a code |
| Conviction | Seeing yourself in the story | Accusing the viewer |
| Finally understanding | A hard question solved by evidence | A confident answer with no evidence |

Sadness alone lowers sharing in the one large study we have (news articles, not video), so heartbreak should resolve into awe or hope.

## Step 3: four tests before production

1. **Retell.** Can someone who heard it once tell it to a friend in two sentences?
2. **Already knows.** What's new for a viewer who knows this story?
3. **Honesty.** Read it flat, with no music. Is the feeling still there, and does the text cause it, rather than our adjectives?
4. **Real people.** Three to five actual people hear the voice take, and say what they felt and who they'd send it to.

AI readers are critics, not an audience. Use them sparingly for confusion, overclaims and insensitive lines. Never use them to rank ideas or to prove a script works, and never cite them as evidence. Challenge your own assumptions rather than seeking agreement from more agents.

## Step 4: learn from real viewers

Log every reel in `system/publish_log.csv`, including its emotional destination.

| Signal | Where | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Skip rate | Instagram Insights | Whether the first image and line work |
| Retention chart | Instagram Insights, YouTube Studio | Which beat loses people |
| Sends per reach | Instagram Insights | Whether question 6 was right |
| Follows per reach | Instagram Insights | Whether question 7 was right |
| Comment types | Read the top 50 | What they actually felt: testimony, correction, question, praise |

**Every 5 reels:** compare what we expected with what happened, then adjust how we choose. Real results outrank AI readers, these notes and Lintel's patterns.

Judge trends, not single reels. Hits are rare: Bible Animations' best Short has about 35 times its median.

## Knowing when to stop

This is a filmmaking process, not a research project. Give an idea a serious creative effort, make a decision, name the weaknesses and produce it. Viewers teach more than another round of analysis.

## Safeguards

- Never invent a detail, a motive or a feeling for a biblical person.
- Never tell viewers what to feel, or describe their spiritual state.
- A word echo alone never carries a climax.
- Never promise what the text doesn't promise. Be especially careful with grief, abuse and illness, and don't turn someone's pain into a lesson.
- A disputed reading is never presented as a definitive discovery.
