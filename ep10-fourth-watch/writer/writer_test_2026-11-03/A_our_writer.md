# Why Would Jesus Walk Past His Own Disciples?

## Narration

The night Jesus walked on water, Mark's Gospel says he meant to walk right past his own disciples. Right after they handed out bread to five thousand, he made them get in a boat. Why walk past them?

Most people picture that boat sinking. That's a different storm. This time they were "making headway painfully, for the wind was against them." From evening to the fourth watch, between three and six in the morning, they rowed about three or four miles. On land, that's about an hour's walk. They had obeyed, and they were barely getting anywhere.

Jesus saw them. He came, walking on the sea. Then Mark writes, "He meant to pass by them." He never explains it. But many readers hear two older stories.

When Moses asked to see God's glory, God said he'd hide him in a rock "until I have passed by." Passing by was how God showed himself. So Jesus may not be abandoning them. He may be showing them who he is.

The second story is harder. Job, who lost his children, said God alone "trampled the waves of the sea." Three verses later: "he passes by me, and I see him not."

In this boat, "they all saw him and were terrified." They cried out, "It is a ghost!" That wasn't a prayer. It was a scream.

"But immediately he spoke to them." "Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid."

God passed by Moses, hidden in a rock. Job said God passes by unseen. Jesus "got into the boat with them, and the wind ceased."

Matthew says they worshiped him. Mark says they still "did not understand about the loaves." "Their hearts were hardened."

He was already in the boat.

## Notes

**Central question:** Why would Jesus walk past his own disciples? It's a real puzzle in Mark's wording that most viewers have never noticed.

**Want and fear:** They want to reach the far shore as ordered. The text records no fear until they see him.

**Ending:** It pays off the hook. He meant to pass by, yet he got in, and Mark says they still didn't understand.

**Interpretation:** "many readers hear" through "who he is" (others say a test, or that it only seemed so); the Job link; "That wasn't a prayer"; the ending's implied point. Mark gives no motive, and the script claims none.
