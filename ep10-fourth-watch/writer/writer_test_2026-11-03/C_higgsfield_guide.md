# He Meant to Pass By

## Narration

Jesus "meant to pass by them." Mark 6 says that about his own disciples, rowing against the wind on the Sea of Galilee.

Three Gospels tell this night, and none says the men in that boat feared the storm. What terrified them was still miles away.

After feeding five thousand men, Jesus "made the disciples get into the boat." From land, Mark says, he saw them "making headway painfully."

By the fourth watch, after three a.m., roughly nine hours of rowing had covered "about three or four miles," an hour's walk.

Then he came "walking on the sea" toward the boat. Only now does any account mention fear: "It is a ghost!"

Near enough to go past, he spoke: "It is I; do not be afraid." In Greek, "It is I" reads "I am."

In Matthew, at the word "Come," Peter "walked on the water." Then "he saw the wind," the only man said to fear it.

Beginning to sink, he cried "Lord, save me," and Jesus "immediately reached out his hand." Those miles had become an arm's length.

Mark never says why Jesus would pass them. Many scholars connect that verb to Sinai, where Moses asked, "Please show me your glory."

What sounds like neglect was God's answer to Moses: "I will cover you with my hand until I have passed by."

Matthew records worship in the boat: "Truly you are the Son of God." Mark says that even this close "their hearts were hardened."

Moses saw only God's back. These men called Jesus a ghost, and "he got into the boat with them, and the wind ceased."

## Notes

Central question: why would Jesus mean to pass men he saw struggling? It is the story's strangest line, and Mark leaves it open, so the script claims no reason. The disciples want the far shore, as ordered. The accounts record their fear only of him, then Peter's of the wind. The ending answers who rather than why: he got in with men who misread him. Through-line: the distance between Jesus and the boat, miles to zero. Interpretation: the Sinai link (flagged "many scholars"; other readings exist), "I am" as more than "it's me," the closing Moses contrast. Nine hours is computed from evening to the fourth watch.
