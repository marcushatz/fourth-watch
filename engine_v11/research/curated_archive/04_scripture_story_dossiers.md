# Original narrative opportunity dossiers — 7 Scripture studies

These are **research briefs, not approved scripts**. They exist to prevent an AI from conflating a compelling *question* with a warranted *answer*. Each dossier has: audience-sized tension; scene evidence; one potentially filmable progression; a provisional payoff; and an epistemic boundary. Primary passages were checked against the ESV in the source register, but any final quote or intertext must be rechecked during script approval. Images and reconstructions must not depict speculation as reported events.

## S01. The fourth watch: the exhausted disciples and the one they did not understand

**Primary:** [Matthew 14:22–33](https://www.esv.org/verses/Matthew%2B14%3A22%E2%80%9333/), [Mark 6:45–52](https://www.esv.org/verses/Mark%2B6%3A45%E2%80%9352/), [John 6:16–21](https://www.esv.org/verses/John%2B6%3A16%E2%80%9321/).

**Human stake:** A group acts on Jesus' instruction, struggles against wind through darkness, and is startled not by an ordinary visitor but by someone walking across water. Fear becomes astonishing recognition. The question that **is** answerable is not “Why did he wait?” but **“What did the disciples still not understand after watching Jesus feed thousands?”** Mark supplies an unusual, direct narrative judgment: they had not understood the loaves, and their hearts were hardened. This unlocks a richer inquiry into recognition and perception without manufacturing a secret motive.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** Jesus requires the disciples to go ahead; he prays apart; wind is against them; Mark says he sees them making painful headway; he approaches during the fourth watch; they think he is a ghost and cry out; he reassures them; in Matthew Peter walks, fears, sinks and is immediately caught; the wind ceases when they board; Matthew records worship and the “Son of God” confession; Mark unusually explains astonishment via their failure regarding the loaves; John says the boat reached its destination immediately after they welcomed Jesus.

**Interpretive opportunities (T2/T3):** An emphasis on the identity of Jesus is grounded in the sequence and Matthew's confession. A link between sea-walking, divine prerogatives, and the “passing by” language of Job 9 or Exodus 33 is discussed by commentators, but remains an interpretation. The text does not explain *why* he chose the fourth watch, whether he engineered the storm, how he saw from shore, how Peter physically returned to the boat, or exactly what each disciple thought.

**Two distinct narrative spines worth testing (not combining automatically):**
- **Experience-first:** After hours fighting the wind, the arrival of help initially frightens them more. Follow one escalating human predicament through reassurance, Peter's rescue and worship. Let the recorded outcome itself carry the climax.
- **Recognition-first:** After feeding the crowd, the disciples are astonished by the sea encounter, and Mark explicitly attributes it to their failure to understand the loaves. Investigate what Mark's juxtaposition asks a reader to reconsider. Keep any proposed theological reading clearly identified as such.

**Visual insight:** A repeated spatial anchor—the boat against the distant shore—can show effort and proximity. Don't mistake dark images and dramatic rain for evidence of a near-sinking disaster: this is **not** the stilling-of-the-storm episode in Mark 4. Avoid a timeline claiming exactly nine hours as Scripture does not specify elapsed hours.

**Weak hook to reject:** “How did Peter get back into the boat?” is answered only at the level of *that* he did, not *how*. “Why did Jesus send them into a storm?” adds an unrecorded intention.

**Test:** After hearing this episode, can someone explain the relationship between the loaves and the disciples' recognition without repeating only a generic “trust God in storms” slogan? If not, the script has not earned its intellectual ambition.

## S02. Hagar: the woman named in the wilderness, the child heard, the eyes opened

**Primary:** [Genesis 16](https://www.esv.org/Genesis%2B16/), [Genesis 21:14–19](https://www.esv.org/verses/Genesis%2B21%3A15%E2%80%9321/).

**Human stake:** A dependent Egyptian servant is treated harshly, flees, and later faces the prospect of losing her son in a waterless wilderness. This is a high-stakes lived predicament that does not need manufactured danger.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** Hagar is named by the narrator in Genesis 16. Sarai gives her to Abram; Sarai subsequently treats her harshly, and she flees. The angel of the LORD addresses her by name, asks where she is going, commands her return, promises descendants, and names her son Ishmael with an explanation about the LORD hearing her affliction. Hagar responds by referring to God as a God of seeing. Years later Abraham sends mother and son away; when water ends, she places the child under a bush and weeps at a distance. God hears the **boy**; the angel addresses Hagar; God opens her eyes to a well, and she supplies water.

**Narrative mechanism:** The paired acts of **hearing** (the explanation of Ishmael's name and the boy heard in Genesis 21) and **seeing** (Hagar's name for God and her opened eyes) are internal textual echoes. They are unusually promising because a final event actualizes earlier language in a new crisis. No speculative Hebrew code is required. Visual identity: name / voice / water / distance / eye-line. A close scene of Hagar unable to watch could be more moving than a montage of extra events.

**Difficult core passage:** Genesis 16:9 commands Hagar to return and submit to her mistress. It must not be silently omitted while selling Genesis 16 as an uncomplicated rescue. A modern narration should clearly avoid implying that a particular command to a biblical person is universal advice to remain under abuse. The passage itself does not include that modern application; an explicit safeguard is the editorial narrator's interpretation and should be reviewed carefully. Don't script Hagar as willingly forgiving Sarai or as experiencing emotional serenity; the text does not report such inner states.

**Additional limits:** A line such as “Nobody ever said Hagar's name” overstates the record: the narrator names her; Abram and Sarai do not use her name in **their quoted speech**. The boy is **not a toddler** in Genesis 21 given the timeline in Genesis 16–21. Also, do not say the well magically appeared; the text says God opened her eyes and she saw it.

**Candidate opening contract:** “What happened to the woman who called God the One who sees?” is answerable. “Why did God send an abused woman back?” is a valid unresolved theological question but cannot be solved by a sentiment at the end.

**Test:** Can the whole narrative preserve the uncomfortable command while making clear what it does and does not instruct current viewers to do? Review with a knowledgeable pastor and someone attentive to coercive or abusive dynamics.

## S03. Peter: two charcoal fires and three questions

**Primary:** [John 18:15–27](https://www.esv.org/verses/John%2B18%3A15%E2%80%9327/), [John 21:9–19](https://www.esv.org/verses/John%2B21%3A9%E2%80%9319/), [Luke 22:54–62](https://www.esv.org/verses/Luke%2B22%3A31%E2%80%9334%3BLuke%2B22%3A54%E2%80%9362/).

**Human stake:** Peter denies his association with Jesus under questioning; the rooster crows. Later the risen Jesus shares breakfast and directly questions Peter about love three times. In Luke, Jesus looks toward Peter after the denial, and Peter weeps bitterly.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** John 18 places Peter by a **charcoal fire** during the denials; John 21 describes **a charcoal fire** at breakfast. Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him, gives instructions involving his flock, and ends with a call to follow him. Luke 22 records the looked-at moment and Peter's grief. These are separate Gospel reports; the script must label sources rather than splice them as if one author supplied every detail.

**Promising spine:** An ordinary object—the charcoal fire—holds two radically different scenes. The first accompanies denial, the second an invitation to service. Unlike a contrived one-word ending, the second fire *carries the remembered past into a changed relationship*. The three questions are not merely a gimmick but Peter's explicit emotional burden: John says Peter was grieved at the third question.

**Interpretive limit:** The text does not explicitly state that Jesus **chose** the charcoal fire to remind Peter of the denial. “John describes both scenes around charcoal fires” is a sourced observation; “Jesus deliberately recreated the courtyard” is speculation. The Greek *agapaō/phileō* switch is a disputed interpretation and should not become a viral hidden-meaning claim without specialist support.

**Candidate ending:** End on the renewed commission and “Follow me,” not a narration imposing a speculative psychological theory. The story's significance comes from what is recorded.

## S04. Mary at the tomb: recognition and commission, not a solved secret of selection

**Primary:** [John 20:1–18](https://www.esv.org/John%2B20%3A1%E2%80%933%3BJohn%2B20%3A10%E2%80%9318/).

**Human stake:** Mary seeks a missing body while grieving, mistakes Jesus for someone else, and recognizes him when he addresses her personally. She then reports what she has seen to the disciples.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** Mary comes early while it is dark; Peter and the other disciple go home; Mary stays weeping at the tomb; she misidentifies the person she sees; he says her name; she recognizes him and is charged with a message.

**Narrative opportunity:** The recognition moment is emotionally complete in a single word. An honest story need not add a bigger fact after it. The outward movement—Mary becomes a messenger—may make a better closing action than reiterating her persistence. The significance is grief transformed into recognition and announcement.

**Interpretive boundary:** John doesn't explain **why** Jesus first appeared to Mary. The longer ending of Mark 16:9 does contain a “first” statement but has well-known manuscript questions; avoid using it as unqualified causal support for an explanatory hook. John 10's sheep recognizing the shepherd's voice is a possible thematic resonance, not an explicit authorial cross-reference in John 20. “It was still dark” does **not** prove the encounter occurred specifically during the fourth watch.

**Hook rule:** “Why didn't Mary recognize Jesus?” might be described but not explained psychophysically: the passage reports the mistake, not the mechanism. Prefer a promise about **what she saw and how the recognition unfolds**.

## S05. Naaman: the powerful man who almost refused the ordinary instruction

**Primary:** [2 Kings 5:1–14](https://www.esv.org/verses/2%2BKings%2B5%3A1%E2%80%9314/).

**Human stake:** A renowned commander needs healing. A captive Israelite girl tells his household where help might be found. Naaman travels with wealth and expectations, but the prophet sends a messenger with an ordinary washing instruction. Naaman almost leaves angry; servants persuade him to obey and he is healed.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** The girl's counsel; Naaman's status and skin disease; his letter and gifts; Elisha's messenger and instruction to wash seven times in the Jordan; Naaman's angry objection that he expected the prophet to appear and act dramatically; servants' challenge; Naaman obeys and is cleansed.

**Narrative engine:** **The proposed solution offended his expectations**, despite being easier than the great deed he had imagined. This creates an unusually teachable reversal without an unrelated cross-reference. A viewer can identify why the commander initially rejects something he desperately wants.

**Visual motifs:** Expensive gifts and royal access contrast with the simple river; powerful commander versus captive girl's first clue; Naaman turning away and turning back. Don't present the girl's captivity as a happy precondition created to assist him: it follows a Syrian raid and remains a painful historical setting.

**Boundary:** Leprosy in English translations refers to a range of skin diseases; avoid modern medical specificity. Do not say that Elisha's instruction was “too easy to believe” as a direct quotation—it is an editorial paraphrase of Naaman's recorded objection.

## S06. Emmaus: a conversation before recognition

**Primary:** [Luke 24:13–35](https://www.esv.org/verses/Luke%2B24%3A13%E2%80%9335/).

**Human stake:** Two disciples leave Jerusalem disoriented by Jesus' death and conflicting news. A fellow traveler asks about their disappointment, then unfolds Scripture; they recognize him at a meal, after which he is suddenly gone.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** They discuss events, express disappointed hopes, explain reports from women at the tomb, hear the stranger interpret Scripture, invite him to stay, recognize him in breaking bread and return to tell others. Luke says their eyes were kept from recognizing him, then opened.

**Narrative spine:** The audience may know who the stranger is, so the suspense is not *who is it?* but *when will they recognize him and what changes when they do?* This is **dramatic irony**, which does not depend on hiding the familiar identity from the viewer.

**Boundary:** The text does not provide the complete content of Jesus' walk-long exposition of Moses and the Prophets. Don't invent a transcript or claim that one specific Messianic prophecy is what opened their eyes. The story contains a natural climax at the table and an immediate outward reaction.

## S07. Naomi: “I came back empty” with Ruth beside her

**Primary:** [Ruth 1:19–22](https://www.esv.org/verses/Ruth%2B1%3A19%E2%80%9322/), [Ruth 4:13–17](https://www.esv.org/verses/Ruth%2B4%3A13%E2%80%9317/).

**Human stake:** Naomi returns after severe loss. She openly speaks of bitterness and emptiness; Ruth, her daughter-in-law, has returned with her. Later the women of the town celebrate the family line and a grandchild, speaking of Ruth's love.

**Recorded evidence (T1):** Naomi's own interpretation of her losses; Ruth accompanies her; Ruth marries Boaz and bears a son; women describe Ruth's love and Naomi's restored household; the genealogy links Obed to David.

**The valuable human paradox:** Naomi says she has returned empty **while the narrative tells us Ruth is beside her**. This is not proof Naomi's grief was unreasonable. It is a dramatic juxtaposition that a film could let the audience perceive without a condescending narrator. The later family scene offers an earned, concrete development.

**Boundary:** Don't say Naomi “didn't appreciate Ruth” or “was lying”—those are projections. Her loss is real. Don't turn the story into a universal promise that all grief will resolve into a child or a marriage.

## Where to begin production research

For **the Fourth Watch debut**, commission **one investigation-first outline** (Mark 6:52 as the surprise) and **one experience-first outline** (fear → recognition). Test which yields a *defensible, substantial change*. Do not force Peter or a Job/Exodus link into either version. Keep Hagar and Peter as promising independent follow-ups, not as detours in the storm video.

Each dossier has sufficient primary evidence for a draft but is **not yet a sermon- or script-approved exegetical treatment**. That distinction is a feature, not a weakness: it prevents the audience-engagement layer from silently claiming theological authority.
