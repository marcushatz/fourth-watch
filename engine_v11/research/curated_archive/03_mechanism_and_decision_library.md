# Story mechanisms: keep, conditional, reject

**Purpose:** turn case studies into choices a writer can actually make. This is a *hypothesis library*, not a viral-writing formula. “Support” means an observable instance in our archived cases, not experimental causal proof. See case IDs in `01_case_index.csv` and sources in `07_source_register.md`.

## Eight strong working mechanisms

| ID | Mechanism | When to consider it | Positive anchor and counterexample | What to do, not merely say | Evidence limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M01 | **Audience-owned tension** | A real person could plausibly ask the question before hearing your video | L01 tree, L02 Cain's wife; versus manufactured “how did Peter return to boat?” | Write what a non-expert already finds difficult, concerning, or surprising; ask someone who hasn't read the research if it matters | Editorial judgment; no audience survey for Fourth Watch |
| M02 | **Operational reasoning** | A proposed answer has consequences that can be tested | L01 removes the tree and traces what else must be removed; BP03 balances faithfulness and readability | Make the chain explicit in ordinary language, show 1–2 concrete consequences, then resolve the original problem | Not appropriate when Scripture does not support the explanation |
| M03 | **Source as turning evidence** | One passage overturns a common assumption | L02 “other children,” Mark 6:52 recontextualizes loaves | Name the mistaken assumption, disclose the relevant phrase, state precisely which conclusion follows and which does not | A clue may still be ambiguous; never present inference as translation |
| M04 | **A character's changing predicament** | A person makes choices or faces progressively higher stakes | BP02 Elijah's episodes; Hagar's two wilderness scenes | State the need; show an action/obstacle; let the consequence change the next scene | Do not narrate thoughts or feelings the text does not reveal |
| M05 | **Early provisional answer, deeper question** | Explainers where withholding everything makes the middle empty | L03 Cornelius then wider scope; BP03 answers translation question by ~5 s then explains trade-offs | Give a meaningful partial answer, then introduce a genuine limit or next implication | Not a fixed 33–50% timestamp formula |
| M06 | **Motif returns with changed meaning** | A concrete phrase/object recurs naturally | Hagar hearing/seeing; BP01 crown; L05 door | Plant motif as ordinary detail, then re-use after the situation changes so it means more | A repeated word alone (“they”) is insufficient payoff |
| M07 | **Screen performs the argument** | The audience needs to see a comparison, sequence or count | BP03 balance/spectrum; BA01 seven signs graphic | Keep spatial positions stable and move objects on the narrated relation; avoid decorative animation | Visual benefit is inferred; no playback A/B |
| M08 | **An ending that completes its contract** | Every standalone Short | BP02 thematic climax and L02 resolved puzzle versus BP04/BA05 unresolved teasers | State in one sentence what viewers now understand, not just what they have seen; end on that | Several lower-view cases serve other editorial goals |

## Six conditional techniques

| ID | Technique | Use if... | Don't use if... | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C01 | Direct spoken question | There is a real objection or uncertainty that can be fairly addressed | You are inventing an unimportant curiosity or implying a known motive absent from the text | L01/L02 work; BP01/BP02 do not need one |
| C02 | A surprise near the end | Later evidence genuinely reinterprets the beginning | You must delay the only useful information or overstate a tiny word | L02 and BA01; abandoned “They.” storm ending |
| C03 | Multiple biblical cross-references | Each citation performs a different inferential job | They are impressive but interrupt the human story | L03 integrates sources; L06 risks excessive switches |
| C04 | Fast visual inserts | Multiple concrete objects or a dramatic transition demand rhythm | They simply create motion with no conceptual change | BA03 collage; BA05 map sequence can become cognitively choppy |
| C05 | Narrator addressing “you” | The scene has earned an authentic personal application | It accuses the viewer or covers thin research with emotional commands | L03 specific gratitude vs L07 more direct devotional work |
| C06 | A Bible story retold from start to finish | Each event changes the predicament or reinforces the theme | The viewer knows it all and nothing changes until a small twist | BP02 effective narrative; weaker storm v1 ordinary recap |

## Six misleading “rules” to delete

- **“The script must start with a question.”** False as a universal: BP01 and BP02 begin with a person/subject.
- **“All retelling is weak; use Bible only as evidence for argument.”** Counterexample BP02.
- **“There must be three/four discoveries.”** No count is justified; one meaningful change can be enough.
- **“The greatest fact must appear in the last 5 seconds.”** BP03 answers early; BP02 announces its thesis early.
- **“Shorts must use 3–5-second cuts.”** Moving elements inside a continuous scene also work; rigid editing can damage a human moment.
- **“If AI critics vote for it, the audience will like it.”** Our previous evaluations were constructed by the same writing system. No predictive evidence.

## A seven-question creative decision method (non-scored)

Before writing any script, answer these **in prose, briefly**. If one answer is missing, investigate it rather than inventing another rubric.

1. **Audience concern:** Why would anyone who doesn't already love this passage care now?
2. **Narrative unit:** Are we telling an event, resolving an objection, revealing overlooked textual evidence, or visualizing an argument? Choose **one primary mode**.
3. **Change:** What will the audience newly understand or feel at the end that they did not at the beginning?
4. **Human stakes:** What does someone in the story risk, need, believe, or do? Is that in the passage, or just our inferred psychology?
5. **Answerability:** Does the opening promise an answer the source actually supplies? If not, adjust the promise, not the truth.
6. **Middle:** At what point does something change (cause, obstacle, assumption, or evidence)? If none does, this may be a synopsis instead of a story.
7. **Last beat:** Does the ending fulfill the opening? A quiet ending is fine if it's *substantial*, not merely subtle.

### Two modes cannot be mashed together without permission from the narrative

**Investigation mode:** The engine is a question; evidence is arranged by its answer. Risk: becomes a lecture or hides uncertainty. Reference: L01, L02, BA01.

**Immersive narrative mode:** The engine is a person under changing circumstances; meaning emerges as events accumulate. Risk: a familiar recap with a pasted moral. Reference: BP02, Hagar.

**Visual-explanation mode:** The engine is a changing relationship on screen; narration clarifies the transformation. Risk: static infographic. Reference: BP03.

A story may contain all three elements. But decide which one owns its **spine**. If the viewer cannot describe the spine in a sentence, the draft is overbuilt.

## Scripture-fidelity contract

**Evidence tiers:**
- **T1 — stated:** Direct report in the passage; script may state plainly with reference.
- **T2 — implied:** A defensible inference from a passage; script may use careful verbs (“suggests,” “appears,” “we're not told exactly”).
- **T3 — proposed reading:** A scholar/theological interpretation that must be attributed and distinguished from the text.
- **T4 — invented or untestable:** A motive, inner monologue, historical fact or hypothetical conclusion not established. Exclude from documentary narration; visually mark any illustrative reconstruction as illustrative.

**Hook honesty rule:** Every main interrogative is tagged as answerable fact, reasonable inference, or open question. Open questions are allowed if the script tells the viewer what's known and remains honest about what's unresolved. They **cannot** promise to reveal an unrecorded secret.

**Source pressure test:** For each external commentator or intertext, ask whether removing it would change the answer. If not, omit. For Greek/Hebrew, consult authoritative lexica or a qualified reader; don't infer meaning from a similar-looking fragment of a word.

**False-confidence alert:** A camera pan over Hebrew text, a dramatic verse card, or an authoritative narrator can make a speculative connection seem conclusive. The degree of on-screen certainty must not exceed the degree of textual support.

## The practical draft gate (no numeric score)

1. Read once with the screen off. State the story's change in one sentence.
2. Interrupt after 15–20 seconds. Is there a genuine stake and forward question, or just names/dates?
3. Read the ending with no music. Does it deserve the buildup?
4. Circle every sentence that depends on unstated motives, imagined emotions, questionable historical details, or theological interpretations. Repair, attribute, or cut.
5. Give the *same spoken version* to 3–5 real listeners. Ask without leading: “What do you think the main question was? What changed? Where did you feel bored or unconvinced? What would you tell a friend?”
6. Permit one substantive and one copy edit after that feedback; if still weak, reconsider the central question rather than produce endless similar drafts.

## Adjacent academic evidence (not platform-specific recipes)

- **Narrative transportation:** Green & Brock (2000) found that immersion in narratives is associated with story-consistent beliefs in experiments. This supports caring about imagery, attention and emotional identification, but does not establish the optimum structure of Christian Shorts. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11079236/).
- **Sharing and arousal:** Berger & Milkman (2012) found that high-arousal emotions such as awe, anxiety and anger are associated with and sometimes experimentally influence sharing of news articles; sadness was associated with less sharing in that setting. This is **not proof** that sadness should be omitted from Bible stories or that a hopeful ending will go viral on Instagram. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353).
- **Suspense:** Lehne & Koelsch (2015) propose that suspense comes from instability, conflict and uncertainty around meaningful future outcomes. Use this as a conceptual check (“what consequential outcome is uncertain?”), not a timing formula. [Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4324075/).

## The defendable Fourth Watch promise

**“Read the biblical story closely enough to see what actually happened—and why it matters.”**

This promise rules out invented secrets, manufactured mysteries, endless text analysis without significance, and emotional appeals detached from recorded events. It does **not** require one house narrative structure. It can accommodate investigations, portraits and cinematic stories.
