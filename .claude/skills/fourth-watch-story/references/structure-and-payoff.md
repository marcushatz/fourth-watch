# Narrative structure and payoff

The story chooses the structure. Lintel's structure is one option, not a formula.

## Forms

| Form | The engine | Best for | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dramatic scene | A person under changing pressure | Hagar, Mary, Joseph | A familiar recap with a moral added |
| Investigation | A real question and evidence | Hard objections and textual problems | A lecture, or hidden uncertainty |
| Visual explainer | A changing relationship on screen | Big ideas with no single character | A static infographic |
| Literary pattern | Something repeated that changes meaning | Hearing and seeing in Hagar | A word echo with no substance |
| Portrait | A life that shows one idea | Elijah | A list of events |

Choose one to own the spine. Others can help, but a viewer should be able to state the spine in one sentence.

## Openings

The first line creates an honest expectation.

| Opening | When it works |
|---|---|
| An action already happening | The event is gripping before it's explained |
| A hard choice | The viewer instantly wonders what they'd do |
| A person in trouble | One specific person can stand for the whole story |
| A mystery | The text truly leaves something strange or unresolved |
| An extraordinary moment | The most startling fact can be stated flat |
| A question | A real person would ask it, and it matters |
| A wrong assumption | People picture it differently from the text |

Avoid opening with definitions, background, names and dates, or a promise the video can't keep.

## The middle

The middle is where weak scripts die.

- Each beat should cause, complicate or deepen the next one. If beats can be swapped, it's a list.
- Give a real answer early when one exists, then let it open something harder.
- Every section pays something and gives a new reason to stay.
- A source earns its place by changing an inference, a stake or a consequence. Remove it and ask what collapses.
- A viewer's next question should be answered, or honestly left open.

## Payoffs

| Payoff | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Climax as ending | The emotional peak is the end | Mary hearing her name |
| Reframe | The ending changes what the opening meant | Mark blaming the bread |
| Motif with new meaning | Something planted returns transformed | Hagar's God of seeing |
| Role change | The viewer's position shifts | The end of Never heard |
| Answer plus deeper truth | The question resolves into something larger | The end of Tree |
| Honest open ending | The story states the limit and lands on what's known | "No Gospel tells us why" |

A quiet ending works only if it carries weight. Test it by asking what it means in plain words. If the meaning is small, the ending is small. A logo or a call to follow is never the payoff.

## Length

No word target exists. Cut anything that doesn't change feeling or understanding. Marcus accepts about two minutes when the story justifies it. Our references range from 60 seconds (Elijah) to 138 seconds (never heard). Runtime estimate: words ÷ 2.6 seconds, or ÷ 2.3 for a slower read.

## The creative plan

Writing and visual direction share this plan, so a story decision reaches the camera without being reinvented.

**Top of the plan:**

- **Spine:** one sentence.
- **The four parts:** desire, obstacle, decision that can't be undone, unexpected outcome.
- **Form and opening.**
- **Emotional path,** with the event behind each change.
- **Understanding path:** before and after.
- **Payoff:** what the ending does.
- **Who would send it, and to whom.**

**Then one row per beat:**

| Beat | What happens | Viewer feels | Viewer understands | Narration | Framing | Timing | Acting |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
