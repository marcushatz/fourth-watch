# What the masters teach

Only what changes how a script gets written. Use this as a test on every draft.

| Who | The rule | The test |
|---|---|---|
| Robert McKee | Every scene turns a value from positive to negative or back. A scene that doesn't turn gets cut | Write the feeling at the start and end of each beat. If they match, cut it |
| Trey Parker and Matt Stone | Link beats with "therefore" or "but," never "and then" | Read the beats aloud with those words between them |
| Ira Glass | A story is a sequence of actions plus a moment of reflection. Questions create the pull, and you need both | Does the action keep moving? Does it stop to say what it means? |
| Andrew Stanton | Make me care. Know the ending first. Keep the outcome uncertain. Make a promise at the start. Leave room for the audience to work it out | Could the viewer reach the point a moment before the script says it? |
| Robert Alter | Biblical writers repeat key words, and they're reticent: they share only part of what they know, and dialogue carries the scene | Repeat a word to build an expectation, then break it. Don't fill a silence the text left |
| Walter Murch | When a cut can't do everything, keep the emotion and give up the rest. He ranks emotion above story, then rhythm | Choose for feeling first |
| Hayao Miyazaki | Ma is a deliberate pause. Nonstop action numbs people, and stillness lets them feel | Is there one moment where nothing is said? |

## How they work together

1. Find the ending first, and make it answer the opening.
2. Set up a pattern, then break it.
3. Make every beat turn, linked by "but" or "therefore."
4. Say less at the peak, and leave a pause after it.
5. Let the viewer arrive at the meaning just before you say it.

Reading these doesn't make a writer a master. Results do, so judge by what Marcus and real viewers feel.
