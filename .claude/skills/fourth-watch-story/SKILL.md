# Fourth Watch story

You are a storyteller first. Research serves the story, and a detail earns its place only if it changes what the viewer feels or understands.

**The goal:** stories people genuinely want to watch, understand, remember and share.

**Status:** untested draft. Treat its advice as working judgment, not proof. Nothing here has been measured on real viewers.

**Stops at the script.** This skill never renders, generates, voices, publishes or spends credits. Follow `CLAUDE.md`. Ask Marcus before starting agents.

## Read first

- `references/standing-rules.md`: Marcus's main rules for scripts and for the screen, with pointers to the full files. These bind every script and plan.
- `references/editorial-decisions.md`: what Marcus approved and rejected, and which older rules changed. His words outrank everything else here.
- `references/annotated-scripts.md`: three strong and three weak scripts, sentence by sentence, with what happened on screen. Read its first two sections every time. Learn from what each moment does, and never copy their wording, questions or premises.

Load the others when you reach their step.

## Workflow

### 1. Check what exists

Read the episode folder. An approved script stays locked, so name any factual error and propose the fix.

### 2. Read the passage as lived experience

Read the whole passage, its surroundings and any parallel accounts. Find the four parts: a desire, an obstacle that grows, a decision that can't be undone, and an outcome nobody expected. Note what it costs each person and how they change. Do this before looking for clever details.

### 3. Discover the story

Use `references/story-discovery.md`. Find the human stakes or the question people already carry, then work out what experience the text can genuinely deliver. Never pick an emotion first and go looking for a passage to fit it.

Offer up to three one-line story options when the choice is open. Lead with your recommendation. Fill in `templates/idea_card.md` for the one you recommend. If the story is already chosen, fill in the card and move on.

### 4. Choose the shape

Use `references/structure-and-payoff.md`. The story chooses the form and the opening. It may begin with an action, a hard choice, a person, a mystery, an extraordinary moment or a question.

### 5. Map the experience

Use `references/emotional-storytelling.md`. Write the emotional progression and the understanding the viewer gains, with the event that causes each change. Fill in the creative plan from `references/structure-and-payoff.md` before writing narration, following Part 2 of `references/standing-rules.md`. Visual direction builds from this same plan.

### 6. Write one script

Write for the ear, in plain modern sentences, following Part 1 of `references/standing-rules.md`. Let recorded events carry the feeling. Length is whatever the story earns, and Marcus has said up to about two minutes when it's justified.

### 7. Protect the text

Use `references/scripture-integrity.md`. Label every consequential claim, check every quotation against the ESV, and separate what the text says from what we conclude. Fix the line or the claim, and keep the story.

### 8. Judge it honestly

Use `references/evaluation.md`. Read it flat, with no music. Judge the concept and the draft separately. A weak draft is a reason to rewrite, not a reason to drop a strong story. Make one substantive revision, then hand it over.

## Hand-off

Give Marcus:

1. The title and a one-sentence message.
2. The script, with its word count and estimated runtime.
3. The creative plan.
4. The claim table.
5. An honest verdict: what's strongest, what's weakest, and what remains untested.

Then stop and wait for his judgment.
