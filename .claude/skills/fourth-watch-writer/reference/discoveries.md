# What counts as a reveal (steps 1 and 2)

A reveal is a true thing from the text that **changes what the viewer understood**, and it matters because it touches the struggle the episode opened on. Write each one with a before and an after. If the after equals the before, it isn't a reveal.

`EDITORIAL.md` lists nine kinds of value. These are the forms they take in a script.

## The kinds that work

| Kind | What it looks like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The overlooked detail** | A clause most readers skip | God's first response to Elijah is sleep and food: "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you" (1 Kings 19:7) |
| **The order of events** | When something happens changes what it means | God makes them clothes (Genesis 3:21) before he sends them out (3:23) |
| **The reason the text gives** | A decision explained by the text itself, not by us | Jonah gives his own reason for running: "for I knew that you are a gracious God" (Jonah 4:2) |
| **The apparent contradiction** | Two texts that seem to clash, resolved by reading precisely | A serpent image against "you shall not make... bow down... serve" (Exodus 20:4-5) |
| **The later look-back** | A later book names or quotes the earlier event | Hezekiah breaks Moses' serpent (2 Kings 18:4); Jesus names it (John 3:14) |
| **The same person, changed** | Someone returns, and the change is the point | Judah proposes selling Joseph (Genesis 37:26-27), then offers to stay as a slave in Benjamin's place (44:33) |
| **The contrast between two people** | Two characters facing the same test | Saul hunts David, and David spares Saul (1 Samuel 24); the son who left and the son who stayed (Luke 15) |
| **The consequence** | What a choice costs, shown plainly | "To dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19) |
| **The reversal of the common answer** | What people usually say, shown wrong or incomplete by the text | "Cain's offering failed because it wasn't blood": Leviticus 2 has grain offerings God accepts |
| **Context the text assumes** | History or a custom from a named source, labeled HISTORY | Said as history ("In that culture..."), never as Scripture |
| **What the text doesn't say** | Correcting an embellishment | Genesis 33 never says "forgave"; the fruit is never called an apple |

## What does NOT count

- **Trivia that doesn't move the story.** A number or a name is not a reveal unless the story needs it.
- **Look-alike links.** "Both have twelve...", "both on a hill...". A cross-reference must pass the **link test**: the later text names or quotes the earlier one, or a mainstream reading links them *and the script says it's a reading*.
- **A shared word.** "Sharing a Hebrew word doesn't prove an intentional connection between the passages" (Marcus).
- **Folk etymology, gematria and "hidden messages"** in names or letters.
- **A scholar carrying the claim alone.** Scripture carries the argument, and history is cited as history.
- **A motive, thought or feeling** the text doesn't give ("Jacob was furious", "God felt...").
- **The famous fact restated** ("Jonah was swallowed by a great fish").
- **A reveal with no stakes.** True and new, but it changes nothing about the struggle or about God.

## Strength test

Answer these in words before building on a reveal. No scores.

- **Surprise:** do most churchgoers already know it? If so, go deeper into the text rather than dressing it up.
- **Load-bearing:** does the story break without it?
- **Relate:** does it change how someone sees the struggle the episode opened on?
- **God:** does it show something true about who God is or what he does?
- **Visual:** can it be shown as a person doing something, an object or a place?

Drop anything that doesn't change the story.
