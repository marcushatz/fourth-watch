# 02 · Story and script: how a Fourth Watch reel earns every second

**Use this when** you write or revise any script. It comes first because Marcus's priority (Round 16, Oct 31) is that the story is good enough for the viewer and never feels slow.

## 1. The viewer's deal

A stranger gives us about 1.5 seconds, then about 2 seconds at a time. In return the reel must give them five things:

1. **A puzzle they already wonder about.** It's a question about a person or object in the Bible, and it has an answer.
2. **The answer, from the text, in a sentence they can repeat.** The hits state it plainly by the midpoint.
3. **At least three things they didn't know.** "Already knew it" is the moment the thumb moves.
4. **A reason to stay every few seconds:** a new fact, a new picture, or a question that has just been opened.
5. **A line worth sending to one specific person.** Two viewer tests of Ep 1 named the same friend: "a friend who leads worship… and says 'God's still blessing it' about stuff they shouldn't be doing."

If a line does none of these five things, cut it.

## 2. The beat sheet (ASK THE TEXT, 90–120 s, 230–300 words)

| Beat | When | What it does | The hit pattern | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 0–5.5 s | Asks the puzzle, naming a Bible person or object, in the third person | "Where did Cain's wife come from?" / "If God knew Eve would eat the fruit, why didn't He remove the tree?" | The question is fully spoken by 5.5 s. There's no "you" or "we" in it. A name or object is in it |
| **Sharpen** | 5–12 s | One concrete Bible fact that makes the puzzle harder or stranger | "If Adam and Eve were the first people…" | Sentence 2 has a name, a number or an object |
| **Who asks** *(optional)* | 1 line | Says who believes or asks it, then pivots into the text | "It sounds convincing until you open Exodus." | One line, never two |
| **The turn** | by ~25 s | "But notice…": the overlooked detail, the decisive verse, or a thought experiment | Top six: 14–25 s, after 0–14 s of story; aim for ≤ ~10 s of story before it | Open on the scene that holds the detail; give earlier events one or two lines |
| **Core answer** | 35–50% of runtime | The answer in the narrator's own plain words, ideally reusing the hook's words | Top six 33–50%; the lows 59–82% | No dripping it out: the first answer piece and the core answer are ≤25 s apart |
| **Evidence** | the second half | 2–4 new beats: another verse showing the same pattern, a little-known detail, a refuted wrong reading ("Some say… The text never says that."), a fair note where Christians differ | Hits: 42–93 s of new evidence after the answer | Each beat is NEW information, never more story and never a recap |
| **Re-ask + answer** | 75–90% | The hook's own words again, then one line that answers it with the hope or comfort line | All three hits, at 82–89% | Answers the question; fits every viewer; never "come back" or a verdict on the viewer |
| **Closing question** | the last 5 s | One real question tied to the central image | 16–17 words | ≤17 words; a real question; Marcus's approved form is a choice between options of the SAME TYPE (Round 9–10), shown as answer buttons |

**Word budget:** about 230–300 words.
- Runtime ≈ words ÷ 2.65 + the closing (the question card and logo, about 5–9 s).
- 270 words ≈ 107–111 s; 300 words ≈ 118–122 s; 379 words (Ep 1 v57) ran 149.9 s.
- **Write the answer line before anything else.** List three things the viewer doesn't know that prove it, then draft.

## 3. What makes a reel feel slow (the drag list)

These are the causes found in Ep 1 and in the low-performing reels, with the fix for each.

| Drag | Example (Ep 1) | Why it drags | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Story after the answer** | v57 answers at ~70 s, then ~75 s of story follows | The viewer's question is already answered, so nothing pulls them on | After the answer, only NEW evidence. If the story continues, it must answer a new question the answer just opened |
| **A signpost line** | "But Samson's story doesn't end there." | It announces instead of showing; a first-time viewer named it as a swipe point | Cut it. The cut to the next picture does the job |
| **The drop after the answer** | Ep 2 Jonah v2: "the plant part feels like an epilogue" (two viewers in two rounds) | The question is answered, so the next beat feels optional | One line that promises something *specific* and new, not "there's more". Jonah v3: "And the ending is stranger than the fish." The round 3 viewer: it "is what kept me watching." (A vague signpost still drags; see the row above) |
| **The famous part told again** | Jonah v1: "Then came the storm, the sailors, and three days and three nights in a great fish" ("I'd swipe… the Sunday-school part I already know") | The viewer knows it, so retelling it feels like a recap | Name it as known, in one line, and move on: "You know the next part: the storm, the sailors, the great fish." The round 2 viewer said it "handled that well". Shoot it as a fast run (`06c` §5) |
| **A recap** | "His choices had cost him his eyes and his freedom." (just after "Blinded…") | It repeats what the viewer just saw | Cut it. If the point matters, make it the picture, not the words |
| **An explained contrast** | "Earlier, he had assumed… Now, he asked…" | Both v59 viewers flagged it: one called it the weakest line, the other said it "explains a contrast I could mostly feel already, so it drags a little" | Show the contrast (two panels: wake / pray) and let the words move on |
| **Retelling the famous part** | The Delilah stretch (the bindings, the haircut) | "I know this part already, so my thumb starts moving"; "it turns into the Samson-and-Delilah story I already know" (the two v59 viewers) | Keep the famous part to one or two lines, and lead with what's new about it ("1,100 pieces of silver from EACH lord"; "the exact same words every time") |
| **A quote that proves nothing** | The 16:30 death count | The v57 viewer was confused by it; it adds nothing to the answer | Quote only what moves the answer, and follow every quote with what it proves |
| **A warning aimed at the viewer** | The Matthew 7 "I never knew you" line | Both first-time viewers who heard it called it a threat or a swipe point ("felt like a threat"; "wouldn't send it to a non-church friend") | Let the story carry the warning ("he did not know that the LORD had left him") |
| **Sermon voice** | "You can be gifted, lead, and help people…" | "stop the story to deliver a sermon" (v59 viewer K, on this line next to Matthew 7); "close" to a sermon (viewer X) | At most 3 "you" lines in a script; never describe the viewer's conduct; take-aways in the third person or with "we" (Marcus's comfort lines use "we") |
| **A fact with no why** | "he pulled up the city gate, posts and all" | "I didn't get why it mattered… nobody says that" | Say or show its point: "the same night, his strength still worked" (a pill can carry it) |
| **An abstract answer** | "His faith is what Hebrews names." | "I never saw his faith; I only saw him ask for revenge" | Point the answer at something the viewer saw ("even at his lowest, he could still call on God") |
| **A slow read** | 2.53 words/s over the whole of v57 | Every hit is faster (≥ 2.59 over the whole runtime) | Aim for ≥ 2.6 words/s over the whole runtime (~2.7 over the voice); get pace from shorter lines and fewer pauses, never by stretching words (Marcus's rule) |
| **A long ending** | v57 ran 6.4 s past the last word | The other channels' hits end 0–1.2 s after it | The last word is near the last frame |

## 4. Line craft

1. **Write for pictures.** Every sentence names a person doing a verb or an object that something happens to. If a line can only be a character standing or a card, rewrite it or give it an object (direction_rules §0).
2. **One clause, one picture.** Three verbs in a sentence = three beats. Short clauses also cut cleanly.
3. **Quote, then meaning.** Every quote is followed by one plain line that says what it proves ("So…", "Notice…", "Which means…"). The hits do this 9 times out of 12.
4. **New before known.** When a famous scene must appear, open it on the detail people don't know.
5. **Give names a one-word frame** the first time a viewer could misread them: "the Philistine city of Gaza", "Hebrews 11, a list of people of faith". Or put the frame in an on-screen pill so no words are added.
6. **Plain words** (Marcus): "building", not "house" or "temple"; "to be used to help other people", not "for other people"; no churchy stock phrases; avoid the word "verdict".
7. **Numbers carry weight** when shown: "1,100 pieces of silver from each lord" with a counter.
8. **Lessons after the proof.** The first quotable line comes after the first piece of evidence (the hits' first aphorism lands at 33–45%).
9. **Sentence length:** at most 25 words (ASK THE TEXT). Read every line aloud; if you take a breath mid-line, split it.
10. **Odd-sounding quote words** ("O Lord GOD") stay exact on the card, and the spoken line can stop before them or carry the quote into the card.

## 5. Accuracy (binding; Marcus's rules)

- ESV only, word for word in quotes; quoted verbs must match ("rushed upon him", not "came upon").
- Never state what the text only implies: no "God answered" at the pillars. Show the prayer, then the push.
- Nothing is "last" unless the text makes it last. "Samson's last act began with a prayer" was flagged on Oct 31, because 16:26 has him ask to feel the pillars first.
- No collective description applied to one person (Hebrews 11:33–34 describes the list).
- A count stands only if every item uses the claimed wording.
- Contested questions: say what the text says, then the main views in one line.
- Never assume the viewer has strayed ("how far you've fallen", "calling you back to Him").
- Never mention something the video doesn't show.
- Scripture is the only authority; a named thinker may only sharpen the question, never settle it.

Every script goes through `fw-script-review` (the checker plus four independent reviewers) before Marcus sees it.

## 6. Hooks

**Strong patterns** (third person, a concrete name or object, a puzzle with an answer):
- If [Bible fact], why [puzzling thing about a named person or object]?
- Where did [person or object] come from? / What happened to [person or object] after [event]?
- Why did God [do X] when [Bible fact]? (e.g. "Why did God keep giving Samson his strength while he kept sinning?")
- Was [person] really [common belief]? Only when the answer is definite.

**Weak patterns** (the lows):
- Second person or "we": "Are you…", "Why do we hide…".
- Abstract: "If God is love, why is there a hell?" (194K, a low).
- Hypothetical: "What if Adam and Eve never ate?"
- A reading or devotional with no question.

**Before choosing:** type the hook into YouTube and Google search. If it (or a close variant) autocompletes, people already ask it.

**On screen:** the question is the top caption from frame one, never a static title card. Something visibly changes by 1.3 s, and the first big change lands on the question's key word (direction_rules §3).

## 7. Endings (Marcus's model plus the hit pattern)

1. The answer is already clear before the end.
2. **Re-ask the hook in its own words at 75–90%**, then answer it in one line that carries the hope or comfort and fits every viewer. Marcus: "name both what the story warns and what it gives hope for"; the comfort uses "we", never "you've failed".
3. **The closing question** (Marcus wants one): ≤17 words, tied to the central image. Marcus's approved form (Rounds 9–10) is a real choice between options of the SAME TYPE, shown as answer buttons with a comment being typed. Note for him: both v59 viewers read the three-option Ep 1 question as "engagement bait", so a sharper two-option choice may land better. His call. Any question goes through the fact-check like every other line.
4. **End within ~1–4.5 s of the last word.** The logo is short.

## 8. The writing procedure

1. **Pick the puzzle** (`system/01_topic_selection.md`, `research/episode_bank.md`).
2. **Write the `answer:` line,** then `## Value`: three or more things viewers don't know, each with a reference.
3. **Write the beat sheet** (section 2) as bullet points with the time each beat should land.
4. **Draft the VO** to 230–300 words. Every line carries a [reference].
5. **Self-check against the drag list** (section 3), line by line. Delete before you add.
6. **Run `python3 fw_check.py script episode.md`.** Fix every FAIL, and read the hit-structure WARNs.
7. **Run `fw-script-review`** (the four reviewers). Merge the fixes with Marcus's rules winning.
8. **Run a fresh first-time viewer** on the merged version. If clarity is under 4/5, or the viewer names a swipe point, fix it and test again with a new viewer.
9. **Show Marcus:** the script, the runtime estimate, the change log and at most one decision.

**Lessons for Ep 2 on:**
- third-person hook;
- turn by ~25 s;

- the answer line points at something on screen;

## 10. Pre-flight checklist (answer yes to all)

1. Does the hook name a Bible person or object, in the third person, and is it a question people already search for?
2. Is the question fully spoken by 5.5 s?
3. Does sentence 2 make it sharper with a concrete fact?
4. Does the "But notice…" turn land by ~25 s, after ≤ ~10 s of story?
5. Is the story under 25% of the runtime (never over 40%)?
6. Is the core answer stated plainly at 35–50%?
7. Is everything after the answer new evidence (no recap, no retelling)?
8. Is every quote followed by what it proves?
9. Are there three or more things most viewers don't know, each with a verse?
10. Are there at most 3 "you" lines, none describing the viewer's conduct?
11. Does the hook come back at 75–90%, answered with the hope line?
12. Is the closing question ≤17 words, real, and not homework?
13. 230–300 words; ≥ 2.6 words/s over the whole runtime once recorded?
14. Does every sentence name something that can be shown happening?
15. Do all the drag-list fixes hold (no signposts, recaps, explained contrasts or warnings aimed at the viewer)?
16. Is every line ESV-checked and every implied event shown, not stated?
17. Would a first-time viewer restate the answer, and send it to someone specific?
